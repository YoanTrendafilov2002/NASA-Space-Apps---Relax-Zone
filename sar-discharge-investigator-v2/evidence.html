Read the map

Radar image
Dark gray is a weaker return. Light gray is a stronger return. The legend gives the displayed range in decibels (dB). Transparent gaps within a raster crop have no displayed measurement.
Study area
Dashed lines mark the area of interest. Solid lines mark the selected raster crop when a raster is available. Red is used for oil studies and orange for wetlands.
Review points
A red diamond marks an oil review location. An orange circle marks a wetland review location. Select a point to read why it was marked and open its evidence.
Recognition review outlines
Read the legend for each image. At Eugene Island a solid red line marks measured dark water that agrees with the dashed NOAA outline. At Curuai a solid orange line marks exposed water that radar and an optical image both picked out. Other dashed lines can trace independent reference boundaries.
Radar change
On the optional Radar change layer, orange pixels show stronger returns and olive pixels show weaker returns, by at least 3 dB between dates. These colors describe changes in radar return.
Map background
In the Study palette, green land and blue water provide geographic context. Use the dated radar image and its evidence to examine current conditions.

Compare two dates

  1. Start in Acquisitions. Inspect two measured dates over the same place in turn. Use the same channel, such as HH, on both. Each channel records a particular transmit and receive orientation.
  2. Check the records. In Source audit, check the product, radar frequency, orbit track and pass direction. For numerical comparison, use aligned grids and pixels with valid measurements on both dates.
  3. Use the same display range. A brighter display can result from a different contrast stretch. Use the calibrated values for a numerical comparison.

Try Curuai. Radar change compares 13 August and 12 October 2026 over the common, usable area. Roughly +3 dB means twice the returned power and −3 dB means half. Look to water levels, weather and vegetation for clues about why the signal changed.

Try Qeshm in HH. Acquisitions plots how the typical return changed across the same 1.61 million cells on three measured dates. Select a point to open that image. Values between the observation dates are unknown.

Could a dark patch be oil?

A surface film can smooth the short waves that scatter radar energy. Calm water and naturally occurring films can also make the sea look dark.

  1. Compare the patch with nearby open water from the same pass. Check wind at the time of the image and whether the radar signal is strong enough to interpret.
  2. Look for a separate observation near the same time, such as an incident response report or a slick mapped from another sensor. Match its location and time to the radar scene.

Where might water have changed?

Smooth open water often looks dark. Water among stems or tree trunks can make a stronger return, depending on the plants and the viewing direction.

  1. Start with a mapped wetland boundary. Compare similar radar passes before and during the period you want to investigate.
  2. Check a nearby water-level gauge or same-date optical image for exposed water. Under vegetation or cloud, look for field records or an independent water-level estimate.

When does less water suggest drought?

Wetland extent can rise and fall in a normal year. Compare the same season across several years to establish the usual range. Use rainfall and water-level records to see whether the deficit persisted.

Check the sources

Open Source audit on the map to see the acquisition date, provider, channel and how much of the crop was usable. A measured layer can include a downloadable GeoTIFF. A catalogue entry tells you what the provider lists for that acquisition.

Where Recognition tests appears for a site, open it to see how the research model performed against a separate reference. Ask chat What does dB mean? or What terms are in your answer? for short, sourced explanations.
